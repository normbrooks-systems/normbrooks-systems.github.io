Case study 04

Inventory Control & Root-Cause Analysis

A stock-out is not a cause

In this case study Match the investigation to the consequence Treat the physical inventory as evidence The result Why this still matters to me

One of the habits that shaped the way I approach systems started with a stack of out-of-stock stickers.

At H.T. Hackney / Quality Foods, I would receive stickers for items the previous pick shift had been unable to find. The normal task was straightforward: verify that the product was actually missing and adjust the inventory record.

After doing that repeatedly, I started wondering why the record was wrong in the first place.

Instead of treating every stock-out as a simple shortage, I began looking at item histories. One particularly large discrepancy led me back into the paperwork, where I found a keying error that had corrupted the counts of two different items at once. Correcting the visible shortage would have fixed one number. Finding the transaction error explained why both numbers were wrong.

I started doing that more often.

Within about six months, the ad hoc investigation had turned into a repeatable triage process.

Match the investigation to the consequence

Not every discrepancy deserves the same amount of time.

I eventually settled on three practical levels of investigation.

Under $5

Physically verify that the item is not there. If nothing obvious explains the discrepancy, write it off. The value usually does not justify a paperwork investigation.

$5 to $20

Verify the stock-out, review item history, and check for likely keying, slotting, or paperwork errors. Check the relevant physical locations, but normally do not retrieve older receiving paperwork unless something in the history points there.

Over $20

Perform the same checks, then reconcile the discrepancy against receiving paperwork and the original transaction trail.

The thresholds were not handed down as a formal methodology. They developed from doing the work often enough to learn where additional investigation paid for itself.

The principle was simple: spend human attention where the potential error justifies it.

Treat the physical inventory as evidence

The important shift was conceptual.

A system quantity is not ground truth merely because it is stored in a computer. It is the result of receiving, keying, transfers, adjustments, and other human transactions. Any one of those can be wrong.

When the shelf and the system disagreed, I stopped treating the shelf as the noisy input that needed to be corrected automatically. The disagreement was evidence that something in the chain deserved inspection.

Sometimes the answer was ordinary shrink.

Sometimes the product was in the wrong location.

Sometimes a receiving or keying error had moved inventory from one SKU to another on paper without moving anything physically.

The stock-out was the symptom. The useful work was determining which failure mode had produced it.

The result

During this period, inventory shrink remained below $1,000 against approximately $5 million in inventory across multiple inventory cycles.

That result came from many ordinary controls, not one dramatic intervention. My contribution was to stop treating recurring discrepancies as isolated corrections and start using them as signals about the process that produced the record.

Why this still matters to me

This is one of the earliest examples of the kind of root-cause analysis I still enjoy.

The visible failure is rarely the whole problem.

If an item is missing, adjusting the count restores the record. If the same class of transaction error remains upstream, the system will simply create another discrepancy later.

I would rather understand the failure path, decide whether it is worth chasing, and correct the cause when the evidence supports it.

That does not mean every problem deserves a forensic investigation. The under-$5 threshold was just as important as the over-$20 one. Good analysis also means knowing when the likely value of an answer is lower than the cost of finding it.
