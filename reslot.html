Case study 03

Class 15 Reslot

Designing the department around where the work actually happened

In this case study Movement was highly concentrated The layout itself needed to help the picker The result What survives from the project What this project represents

At H.T. Hackney / Quality Foods, I was eventually given responsibility for reorganizing the Class 15 tobacco department. Someone at corporate showed me how to pull the movement report I would need, gave me some initial guidance on slotting, and then trusted me to work through the reslot.

The surviving planning workbook covers 627 SKUs and includes each item's original slot, nine weeks of movement, recent activity, and proposed new slot.

The department had not been designed as a coherent picking system. Earlier configurations had either attached groups of tobacco inventory to another department in small aisles or stretched the operation into a long path that required unnecessary travel. Keeping product moving routinely required multiple people.

I approached the reslot as both an inventory problem and a movement problem.

Movement was highly concentrated

The nine-week activity data showed that the work was not evenly distributed across the assortment.

A particularly useful concentration emerged in the surviving slotting data: 109 SKUs placed in the even-numbered fast-mover bays from 52 through 68 represented only about 17% of the 627-item assortment, but accounted for roughly 80% of the recorded nine-week activity.

That changed how I thought about the space. If a relatively small set of products generates most of the touches, those products should not be treated like every other item in the department.

The final layout concentrated high-activity inventory into the most accessible area, while slower-moving categories could occupy positions that required more travel. In the surviving reslot data, the areas assigned to cigars, snuff, and the high-movement end of the layout together account for roughly 95% of the recorded activity. The practical goal was to make most routine movement reachable without repeatedly traversing the full department.

The exact point was not to chase a mathematically perfect slot map. It was to put the walking where the work justified it.

The layout itself needed to help the picker

Movement frequency was only one constraint.

The department also had to make sense to a person standing in it.

I grouped inventory by recognizable product families so the physical environment provided clues about where something belonged instead of forcing the picker to rely entirely on arbitrary slot numbers. I also tried, where practical, to avoid putting similarly named, similarly sized, or otherwise easily confused products directly beside one another.

The surviving draft is a working document rather than a perfect final-state record, so I do not claim that every similar SKU was separated. The design principle was to reduce avoidable selection errors without destroying the movement logic that made the department faster to work.

A central cut-through between the rack sections was another important part of the final layout. The previous long-path arrangement forced unnecessary end-to-end travel. The opening near the middle allowed the picker to move between sides without walking the entire length of the department every time the work crossed over.

That physical change mattered because warehouse efficiency is often less about how far apart two slots are on paper than about the route a person must actually walk to get from one to the other.

The result

After the reslot, labor hours in the department dropped substantially.

A process that had routinely required multiple people to keep tobacco moving could normally be handled by one person after the redesign.

The improvement did not come from asking anyone to move faster.

It came from reducing the amount of unnecessary movement and unnecessary mental translation built into the work.

What survives from the project

Two useful artifacts remain from the project.

The reslot workbook preserves the SKU-level planning data: 627 products, their historical movement, and the proposed new locations.

The final Class 15 layout shows how the categories were physically arranged, including the fast-mover area and the central break in the rack structure.

I do not have a formal "before" drawing. The earlier layouts survive mostly in memory, so I do not reconstruct one and present it as historical evidence. The final plan and the movement data are enough to show the logic of the redesign without pretending the documentation is more complete than it is.

What this project represents

I did not know the formal industrial-engineering language for what I was doing at the time.

I had data, some guidance, a department that took too many people to keep moving, and enough familiarity with the work to see where the friction lived.

The reslot combined movement frequency, product family, walking distance, physical access, and pick accuracy into one practical layout.

That combination matters more to me than any one optimization. A layout that minimizes walking but makes products easier to confuse is not a good layout. A layout that is beautifully categorized but forces the picker to walk the full department for routine work is not a good layout either.

The system had to work for the person inside it.
