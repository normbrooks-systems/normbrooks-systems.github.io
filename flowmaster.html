Case study 02

FlowMaster Process Optimization

The machine already knew how to solve the problem

In this case study The missing capability was not actually missing From delayed inspection to immediate containment The larger constraint was human pacing Why I still use this example

One of the most consequential process improvements I made in direct-mail production started with an extremely simple problem: a high-speed automated process was being paced by a person opening envelopes by hand.

The job used personalized letters, personalized reply pieces, and personalized carriers. The FlowMaster inserter could perform multiple barcode reads and stop on a mismatch, but the carrier itself was not part of the automated match sequence. A person at the end of the line periodically opened finished envelopes and checked that the name on the carrier matched the material inside.

The manual check worked, but it imposed two constraints.

First, manual verification created a throughput ceiling. The machine had to be paced around the person checking it rather than around its own mechanical capability.

Second, the check detected errors after they had already happened.

If the machine lost sequence, several minutes could pass before the mismatch reached the person checking finished pieces. Production during that interval could leave a batch of sealed envelopes that had to be opened, inspected, corrected, and resealed.

The check did not prevent the error. It only told us, late, that an error had occurred.

The missing capability was not actually missing

The carrier already had a 2D Data Matrix code on it.

The FlowMaster already had cameras.

The FlowMaster already had matching logic.

The FlowMaster already knew how to stop when two reads did not agree.

The problem was physical: the existing camera position could not reliably see the carrier code where it passed through the machine.

The solution was not elegant fabrication. It was a jury rig.

I mounted a camera using a small collection of hardware - a flat support, a strap holder, a screw, and a right-angle bracket - so the reader could see the carrier's existing code. I then used the machine's existing read-and-match capability to include that carrier in the verification sequence.

No new matching software was required. The system already knew what to do once it could see the information.

From delayed inspection to immediate containment

After the change, a mismatch stopped the FlowMaster at the offending sequence.

The operator could remove or reposition the affected material, restore the correct order, and resume production.

This did not reduce the underlying frequency of sequencing errors. That is an important distinction. The improvement was in detection and containment.

Before the change, a sequencing error could remain invisible until a human found a mismatched name downstream. By then, a large block of finished mail might be suspect.

After the change, the same class of error became an immediate machine event.

That converted a potentially large rework problem into a local correction.

The larger constraint was human pacing

The quality improvement mattered, but the larger operational effect came from removing the manual inspection step as the speed governor.

This particular job had been spread across three FlowMasters. Staffing a machine normally required at least two people if the process was going to move at a useful rate. In practice, the work consumed a little more than two machines per shift on average, tying up both equipment and people while other jobs waited behind it.

Once the carrier could be verified in line, the machine no longer had to operate at a pace set by someone opening and checking finished envelopes.

The result was a major release of capacity. Depending on the shift and how the job was being staffed, roughly one to five people could be reassigned to other work. Machines that had been occupied by the matching job became available for production that had been blocked behind it.

The second- and third-order effects mattered more than the bracket itself. The capacity released helped eliminate mandatory overtime affecting six shifts.

Why I still use this example

There is nothing technically glamorous about the physical modification.

That is why I like the example.

The value was not in designing a complicated new machine. It was in asking a different question about the process.

The process could be approached as a staffing problem: a person has to verify the carrier, so how do we keep the verification step from falling behind?

I found a different question more useful: Could the machine verify the carrier itself, since the carrier already had a machine-readable code and the machine already compared reads?

Once the problem was stated that way, the remaining obstacle was geometry.

A few pieces of mounting hardware changed where the camera could look. That small physical change converted a downstream manual inspection into an immediate machine interlock, reduced the consequences of sequencing errors, removed a human pacing constraint, and released labor and equipment for the rest of the operation.

That is the kind of process improvement I look for: not necessarily the biggest intervention, but the intervention aimed at the actual constraint.
