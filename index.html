For ten years I was an operator on the floor. The work that ended up mattering most happened around that job, not because of it. The first piece, in 2019, was a Pitney Bowes FlowMaster envelope inserter running a carrier-matched job at 4,000 pieces per hour — about a third of what the machine was rated for — because that was as fast as the human at the QC seat could keep up. Accepted wisdom held that this specific machine couldn't read carriers in line. Accepted wisdom turned out to be inherited, not engineered.

Method · FlowMaster carrier-read SOP, 2019

What conventional wisdom didn't know about the machine.

Read the method Hide method

  1. Notice the rule the role had inherited.

    The handbook said "check every five pieces" at the QC seat. The intent was a time-based sample — pop a wet flap, verify the insert against the carrier, reseal before the glue cured. In practice, operators who couldn't hit the three-second loop drifted into reading "every five" as a position count, grabbing whatever piece sat five slots down the belt. By then the glue had set and the check became a fight against a sealed envelope.

  2. Cost out the actual failure mode.

    When the inserter desynced from the carrier feed, recovery wasn't "pull the bad piece." It was working backward through the sealed pieces on the sort table, opening each one without destroying the carrier, and re-pairing inserts to recipients. A 50-to-60-piece reconstruction was a 30-to-40-minute floor stop. I had a Pilot Precise V5 with the print worn off the barrel from being used backward as a flap lifter; I still have it.

  3. Look at what the machine already had.

    The FlowMaster carried three cameras for inline barcode reads, a mismatch-stop circuit, and an onboard encoder that put the carrier at a known position every cycle. The carriers themselves had a 2D barcode in the upper-right corner — the spot the stamp would later cover — readable from below as the piece passed face-down through the chain conveyor. Everything needed to read the carrier in line was already on the machine. The "you can't read a carrier on a FlowMaster" answer had hardened into something nobody had re-examined.

  4. Two hours, one mount, one camera moved.

    Walked up to the supervisor and said I'd beat first and second shift's combined number by end of shift if he gave me two hours. He gave them to me because the trust ledger was real. Jury-rigged a mount under the carrier conveyor, relocated one of the three cameras with its trigger and data cables, configured it against the existing match-stop, ran test pieces, started the run. No new software. The configuration files already supported what I needed.

  5. The cascade matters more than the multiplier.

    The 4× line throughput is the part that fits on a resume; it's downstream of removing the human-pacing constraint, not of any speed improvement. Within twelve hours the scheduler saw what one machine was now doing and reduced the job from three FlowMasters to one. Two machines and five operators freed for other work. Weekend overtime cleared. The machine's mechanical capability didn't change — the constraint downstream of it did, and the schedule reorganized around the new reality.

In October 2024 I moved off the floor into Production Supervisor over the night shift. The line climbed steadily from there — by February it was holding a sustained 20% production uplift, and it kept holding through September. The week I stopped being the supervisor, the number more or less stopped too.

The other thing that came out of those months was the dashboard — January 2025, in Google Sheets. Date-partitioned data, compound-key joins, runtime schema resolution. The kind of thing that should live in a real warehouse, but the spreadsheet was what management would actually open every morning. They still do.

Counting variance, in most distribution centers, is treated as a fact of life. You patch the output and move on. I started reading variance the other direction — as an error message from a data pipeline upstream of the warehouse floor. Receiving, PO, billing. Each off by something small, in a specific way.

We built a forensic tracing methodology that fixed the inputs instead of massaging the outputs. The accuracy number stopped moving. Audits stopped being events.

Method · Forensic variance tracing

Treating the shelf as the last honest narrator.

Read the method Hide method

  1. Flip the ground truth.

    Textbook approach treats the system record as truth and the count as the noisy thing being verified. I worked the opposite way. Receiving paperwork, POs, billing records, and item masters are all claims — entered by humans, in a hurry, often by humans who hadn't handled the physical product. The shelf is the integrator. When physical and system disagreed, my first assumption was that something upstream had told the system a lie and the count was the first place we'd noticed.

  2. Trace contradictions back through the pipeline.

    For each variance event, I walked the affected SKU back through receiving documents, the originating PO, the matching billing record, and the item master. Records get cross-checked against each other, not just against the count. Most errors clustered at one or two predictable handoffs.

  3. A worked example: 1,800 cartons of nothing.

    System said sixty half-cases of Newport 100s — eighteen hundred cartons. Picking went looking and found zero. Tracing backward: a pallet of a cheaper brand had been received on a delivery and keyed under the Newport SKU at data entry. One keystroke desynced two products at once — the Newports we thought we had, we never received; the cheaper brand the system didn't know about sat in the bin under the wrong name while we kept reordering more of it. Conventional cycle-counting would have surfaced the discrepancy eventually. By "eventually" we'd have run out of Newports on live customer accounts. Both halves of the error fell out of the same upstream event.

  4. Fix the input, not the output.

    Standard remediation is to adjust the inventory record to match the count and move on. Instead we fed the trace back upstream — corrected receiving practice, corrected data-entry checks at intake, corrected the billing reconciliation step. Each fix removed a class of variance, not a single instance. Patching outputs is infinite work; fixing the input that produces wrong outputs is bounded work.

  5. Hold it at 99.98% across multiple cycles.

    Sustained on $5M of stock across multiple six-month audit cycles — with peak cigarette inventory running around two million on its own. The number wasn't the goal. The goal was that audits stopped being events anyone had to prepare for.

The second project that year was a department reorganization — four operators consolidated into a one-person workflow by laying out the inventory for visual identification and a sane pick route.

Same throughput. Same accuracy. A quarter of the labor. Nobody had looked at the floor plan as if it were a data structure before.

About

The shape
of the work.

A short bio, and a note about the family name on the door — since you've probably already noticed it.

I'm a systems-and-operations person who tends to get hired floor-level and ends up rebuilding the spreadsheet that runs the building.

My instinct is to treat operational problems as data problems first. When the warehouse can't agree with its own books, when the line bottlenecks on a step that everyone agrees is fixed, when the spreadsheet management uses every morning was last edited three years ago — those are the situations I do my best work in. The throughput and accuracy numbers on this page are downstream of one habit: I treat variance as a question, not a tax.

I work across systems engineering, process optimization, applied LLM tooling, and embedded hardware. The connecting tissue is schema design and a stubborn belief that most "intractable" problems are unexamined.
