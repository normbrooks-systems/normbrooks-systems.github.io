My career does not read like a conventional engineering or analytics ladder.

I spent most of it close to the work: warehouses, production equipment, operators, supervisors, inventory, schedules, and the small failures that accumulate when a process has grown around its own workarounds.

That background is important to me, but I am not looking to return to a role where my primary value is being production labor. I am comfortable getting hands-on to learn a process because I do not believe you can improve work you do not understand. The distinction is simple: I want to work on the process, not be the process.

The systems work emerged gradually, long before I had a title that described it.

An early mechanical foundation

My mechanical background started before my professional career.

I grew up around race-car fabrication and spent substantial time in a fabrication shop during childhood and my teens. My role was mostly helper-level work: holding and positioning parts, fetching tools, cleaning components, assisting with some reassembly, and watching cars and components take shape from raw material.

I do not present that as ten years of professional fabrication experience. It was not an apprenticeship, and I was not the person doing the welding or machining. What it gave me was prolonged exposure to how physical systems go together and a mechanical vocabulary that later became useful when I started solving production problems as an adult.

Racing was part of that environment as well. My grandfather, Earl Brooks Sr., raced in NASCAR's top division. That history remains personally meaningful to me, but it is background rather than a professional credential. My own work has to stand on its own.

2005 – 2013

H.T. Hackney / Quality Foods

I joined Quality Foods in 2005 and gradually moved through operations roles: operator, truck-loading work, loader, receiving clerk, and eventually assistant first-shift supervision.

Hackney is where the analytical pattern started becoming visible.

One example began with routine stock-outs. Instead of simply confirming missing inventory and adjusting the count, I began tracing discrepancies through item histories and, when the value justified it, back through receiving paperwork. Over time that became a value-based investigation process that helped distinguish ordinary shrink from keying, receiving, and location errors. During that period, shrink remained below $1,000 against approximately $5 million in inventory across multiple inventory cycles.

During Hackney's transition from Retalix to its custom System i environment, I created a document I called System Notes. It grew into a nine-page task-oriented guide covering the operations people actually needed to perform: item lookup, inventory adjustments, movement history, cycle counts, receiving, purchase orders, replenishment, shelf labels, printer queues, and other daily functions.

I also recreated operational forms that had degraded through so many generations of photocopying that they had become difficult to read. It was not glamorous work. It was simply unnecessary friction that could be removed.

Eventually I was given responsibility for the Class 15 tobacco reslot after receiving some guidance from corporate on the movement report and slotting process. That project became one of the clearest early examples of how I work: use the data, learn the physical process, and design the environment around what people actually have to do. The resulting layout compressed a routinely multi-person operation into work that could normally be handled by one person.

My growth at Hackney was gradual. The titles changed slowly; the scope of the problems I was willing to investigate kept expanding.

2014 – 2026

High-Volume Direct-Mail Production

I joined the company as a temporary worker in 2014 and became a full-time operator in 2015. I spent the next several years learning high-volume direct-mail production from the floor.

That long operator period gave me deep familiarity with the equipment, failure modes, job structures, and the difference between a process on paper and a process during a real shift.

Some of the most useful improvements came from questioning assumptions that had become part of the job.

In 2019, a personalized matching process depended on a person opening finished envelopes and checking that the carrier matched the contents. The FlowMaster already had the cameras and matching logic needed to automate that verification; the carrier's Data Matrix code was simply in a position the reader could not see. I improvised a camera mount, brought the carrier into the existing match sequence, and turned a delayed manual check into an immediate machine stop on mismatch. The change freed people and machines for other work. The capacity released helped eliminate mandatory overtime affecting six shifts.

I did not begin deliberately pursuing formal career advancement there until 2024.

Once I did, the progression was fast.

I moved from operator to Production Supervisor in October 2024. In January 2025, while supervising the night shift, I built the first version of the production dashboard that would eventually expand across departments and facilities. During the supervisor period, night-shift production rose and held at roughly 20% above the baseline I inherited, driven by the broader operating system around staffing, scheduling, expectations, and visibility rather than by any one tool.

By late 2025, I was working as Production Analyst (actually titled Production Coordinator), with an increasing share of my development work centered on production analytics, internal tooling, process improvement, cross-facility adaptation, and more automated ways to collect production data.

The dashboard had started as a two-week Google Sheets build. It evolved into a configurable production-management system with daily production calculations, control layers, floor dashboards, historical operator/machine analysis, shift-planning tools, attendance/call-out visibility, and automated initialization of daily assignments.

One supporting tool, the Planner, allowed supervisors to configure operator-to-machine assignments and then transfer those selections into the starting values of the daily production sheets. It was also designed to be printable, giving the shift a practical working document during the existing two-hour capture window.

In December 2025, I traveled to the company's Iowa facility to help configure the Planner and related support sheets for that operation. The trip was part of the broader cross-facility adaptation of tooling that had begun as a local solution.

I also prototyped a Raspberry Pi 4-based production counter and continued developing a machine-side operator concept intended to automate more of the production-capture workflow. In parallel, I explored whether modern commodity hardware could provide a lower-cost alternative for some machine-vision functions.

Development continued into my final weeks with the company. The automated production-capture and integration work did not reach production deployment, so I present those pieces as development work rather than finished implementations.

2026

An intentional reset

After more than a decade in direct-mail production, I chose to make 2026 an intentional reset rather than immediately jumping into the next conventional role.

I have used the time to recenter, work on independent technical projects, and think more deliberately about what I want the next stage of my career to be.

That has included embedded computing, machine vision, automation, data systems, rapid prototyping, and continued work on the kinds of small systems that let me understand a technology from the inside rather than only reading about it.

Photography also remains one of my longstanding personal interests.

My education sits alongside that practical background: I earned an associate degree in Business Management from Miller-Motte Technical College in Lynchburg, graduating summa cum laude with a 3.95 GPA.

What I carry forward

The common thread across these jobs is not a particular software package or manufacturing process.

It is the way I approach problems.

I tend to start with root-cause analysis: what is actually producing the failure or wasted capacity? Then I look at the people, information, equipment, and constraints around it. Sometimes the useful intervention is analytical. Sometimes it is physical. Sometimes it is a spreadsheet, a procedure, a layout change, a piece of documentation, or a bracket made from whatever hardware is available.

I think of that as human-first systems design. The goal is not to remove people from every process. It is to stop asking people to compensate for work the system could handle better, and to make the information they do need easier to act on.

That is the kind of work I want more of: continuous improvement, process engineering, operational excellence, systems or operations analysis, technical project management, operations consulting, and other roles where understanding the real process is the beginning of the job rather than the end of it.
